# Leadership and Service Statement

**Bobby Hodgkinson**  
**Working draft — August 31, 2026**  
**Target: no more than 1,500 words**

I understand leadership as the work of creating durable capacity: aligning people, tools, structures, and shared purpose so that a community can accomplish more than any individual could alone. My leadership began with the laboratories, curriculum, and infrastructure of Smead Aerospace Engineering Sciences and has expanded across CU Boulder, the CU System, professional teaching communities, and public conversations about generative artificial intelligence and engineering education. Across those settings, the consistent thread is translation—turning emerging technologies and educational ideas into practices that colleagues, students, and communities can use responsibly. This work extends the commitment in my 2023 leadership statement while moving from primarily departmental service toward broader institutional and professional impact.[^1]

## Building departmental capacity

Within the department, my service is closely connected to experiential learning. I have contributed through curriculum and operations governance, course and laboratory planning, search leadership, technical mentoring, and the development of learning spaces and equipment. Earlier work included transitioning the quadrotor laboratory into junior vehicle dynamics, improving the spin-module platform, planning the PILOT laboratories, building vendor relationships that expanded access to current equipment, and chairing a Teaching Assistant Professor search that concluded with a successful hire. More recent work includes sustained curriculum development and implementation across ASEN 2802, ASEN 1030, ASEN 2502, ASEN 3501, capstone, and other laboratory courses.[^2]

The most important outcome is not a list of committee memberships or devices. It is a durable instructional environment. My leadership of the core laboratories gives faculty and students access to maintained hardware, software, assignments, testing practices, and shared digital materials rather than requiring each instructional team to rebuild them. Learning assistants and student developers participate in that process, gaining technical and leadership experience while expanding what the team can deliver. My central curriculum contribution has been to provide substantial architecture, content development, implementation, and continued refinement—and to help bring ASEN 2802, ASEN 1030, and ASEN 3501 from plans into sustained educational practice.

My departmental service also includes developing people. I have supervised learning-assistant teams, mentored student developers and SPUR/UROP researchers, advised Sigma Gamma Tau and the Hyperloop project, supported capstone teams, and assisted colleagues with laboratory and instructional systems.[^3] This work is most valuable when a student or colleague moves from receiving help to exercising independent judgment and contributing back to the community. Leadership is durable when the work no longer depends on the person who initiated it.

## Leading responsible AI work across CU

As my classroom work with AI continued to mature, colleagues increasingly asked how the same principles could apply in other contexts. I have served as a Center for Teaching and Learning AI Literacy Ambassador, participated in the Academic Technology Advisory Group and AI/DBER literacy work, contributed to early interdisciplinary AI credential planning through Students as Partners, and helped connect international AI-literacy work to CU conversations. In each role, I have tried to move discussion beyond either enthusiasm or prohibition and toward practical questions: What learning goal are we serving? What should remain a human decision? How will we test quality? What agency should students retain?

That translation work contributed to the CU training required for access to ChatGPT Edu. Working with approximately six faculty and staff colleagues across CU Boulder and CU Denver, I provided technical insight, structural content, and general AI-literacy material for student and faculty/staff versions of the training. At initial deployment, the training had reached more than 1,000 students and several dozen faculty and staff.[^4] This effort extended my impact beyond an individual course, department, and campus by helping establish a shared foundation for responsible and effective use across the CU community.

My educational tools have also transferred beyond aerospace. My explanation-centered chatbot platform and feedback methods were used in MATH 1151 and LAWS 5064, producing nearly 300 unique interactions in Spring 2026. These collaborations matter because they test whether the underlying pedagogy—eliciting explanation, critique, and understanding rather than simply generating answers—can travel across disciplines. The 2026 CU System AI Recognition Award in AI for Teaching and Learning and CU Innovation and Efficiency Top Finalist recognition provide external confirmation that this work contributes to institutional priorities.[^5]

## Building professional communities and sharing practice

My professional service combines scholarship, community building, and translation. Through the Rocky Mountain Artificial Intelligence Interest Group, I serve on the executive board and lead the education subgroup, creating a regional forum in which educators and practitioners can examine AI use with both ambition and rigor. My ASEE papers and presentations document deployed teaching practices, including human-reviewed feedback, comparative evaluation of AI-generated engineering work, concept-feedback failure analysis, and the design of AI-integrated computing education.[^6] I have also contributed professional service through ASEE review and conference activity.

Invited dissemination has allowed the work to reach additional audiences. At the June 2026 Kiewit Faculty Open House, I spoke with approximately three dozen faculty from around the United States about translating AI-in-education deployments into civil-engineering and construction-management programs. At KAIST, I presented collaborative spin-module work as an international example of experiential laboratory development.[^7] Additional talks, workshops, podcasts, and professional conversations have moved lessons from local implementation into broader discussions of engineering education.

The point of dissemination is not visibility alone. I share methods, limitations, and failures so that others can make better decisions. For example, our early concept-feedback work showed that some AI outputs were unreliable; documenting that failure was as important as reporting later improvements. Responsible leadership requires making uncertainty visible, preserving human oversight, and resisting the temptation to present every prototype as a success.

## Extending expertise into the community

I also see public AI literacy as an important form of service. In November 2025, I led “Navigating AI: Build Your Parent Playbook” with the Parent Engagement Network, combining an introduction to generative AI and its implications for parenting with a two-hour guided session using tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, and NotebookLM. I later offered related programming at the Louisville YMCA and have spoken with communities including Balfour retirement residents, Women of Aeronautics and Astronautics, and Front Range Community College.[^8]

My participation in AI advisory groups connected to Boulder Valley School District and the St. Vrain Valley Schools Innovation Center brings a higher-education perspective to local decisions about AI and learning. Through Science Discovery, I mentored two high-school students as they used frontier chatbots while working through an advanced aeroelasticity laboratory. They examined both where an AI partner could help and where limited prior knowledge made that partnership unreliable, then presented their work. The outreach experience became an inquiry into responsible learning itself.[^8]

## Leadership as accountable translation

The scale of my service has changed since my 2023 statement—from primarily departmental curriculum, laboratories, and pandemic response to work that now connects Smead, CU Boulder, the CU System, regional and national teaching communities, schools, and public audiences. The underlying commitment has not changed. I lead by building and deploying practical systems, convening people around shared problems, and helping communities move from abstract concern to informed action.

My next stage of leadership is to make these efforts more durable and measurable: preserve evidence of adoption and outcomes, deepen cross-unit partnerships, strengthen regional educator networks, and continue developing students and colleagues who can lead independently. Artificial intelligence is changing engineering practice and education quickly. My service is aimed at helping our institutions respond with confidence but not complacency—with innovation grounded in evidence, responsibility, student agency, and the enduring human purposes of education.
