# Leadership and Service Statement

**Bobby Hodgkinson**

**Working draft — September 8, 2026**

**Target: no more than 1,500 words**

I understand leadership as creating durable capacity: aligning people, tools, structures, and shared purpose so a community can accomplish more than any individual could alone. My work began with the laboratories, curriculum, and infrastructure of Smead Aerospace Engineering Sciences and has expanded across CU Boulder, the CU System, professional teaching communities, and public conversations about generative AI (AI) and engineering education. The consistent thread has been turning emerging technologies and educational ideas into responsible practices that others can use. Increasingly, I organize that work around a larger goal: building the human and technical capacity to make large courses feel personal.[^1]

## Building departmental capacity

Within the department, my service remains closely connected to experiential learning. I have contributed through curriculum and operations governance, laboratory planning, search leadership, technical mentoring, and the development of learning spaces and equipment. This has included improving the spin-module platform, planning the PILOT laboratories, expanding equipment access through vendor relationships, chairing Teaching Assistant Professor searches that concluded with successful hires, and developing curriculum across ASEN 2802, ASEN 1030, ASEN 2502, ASEN 3501, capstone, and other laboratory courses.[^2]

The most important outcome of this work is not any single laboratory or course. It is the instructional capacity that remains after the initial development is complete. My leadership of the core laboratories has helped create maintained hardware, software, assignments, testing practices, and shared digital materials that future instructional teams can build from rather than recreate. Learning assistants and student developers also gain technical and leadership experience through that process. My central curriculum contribution has been substantial architecture, content development, implementation, and continued refinement, helping bring ASEN 2802, ASEN 1030, and ASEN 3501 from plans into sustained educational practice.

I have also supervised learning-assistant teams, mentored student developers and SPUR/UROP researchers, advised Sigma Gamma Tau and the Hyperloop project, supported capstone teams, and assisted colleagues with instructional systems.[^3] I see these efforts as part of the same responsibility. Leadership becomes durable when students and colleagues move from receiving support to exercising independent judgment, taking ownership, and contributing back to the community.

## Leading responsible AI work across CU

As my classroom work with generative AI matured, colleagues increasingly asked how the same ideas could apply elsewhere. I have served as a Center for Teaching and Learning AI Literacy Ambassador, participated in the Academic Technology Advisory Group and AI/DBER literacy work, contributed to early interdisciplinary AI credential planning through Students as Partners, and connected international AI-literacy work to CU conversations. In those settings, I try to move the conversation quickly from the novelty of the technology to practical educational questions: What learning goals are we serving? What should remain a human decision? How will we evaluate quality? What agency should students retain?

That work contributed to the CU training required for access to ChatGPT EDU. Working with approximately six faculty and staff colleagues across CU Boulder and CU Denver, I provided technical insight, structural content, and general AI-literacy material for both student and faculty/staff versions of the training. At initial deployment, the training had reached more than 1,000 students and several dozen faculty and staff.[^4] The importance of this effort was its reach. It extended lessons learned through local experimentation into a shared foundation that could support responsible and effective use across the CU community.

My educational tools have also transferred beyond aerospace. My explanation-centered chatbot platform and feedback methods were used in MATH 1151 and LAWS 5064, producing nearly 300 unique interactions in Spring 2026. These collaborations help test whether the underlying pedagogy, eliciting explanation, critique, and understanding rather than simply generating answers, can travel across disciplines. The 2026 CU System AI Recognition Award in AI for Teaching and Learning and CU Innovation and Efficiency Top Finalist recognitions provide external confirmation that this work is contributing to broader institutional priorities.[^5]

## Building professional communities and sharing practice

My professional service combines scholarship, community building, and the sharing of practices that others can adapt. Through the Rocky Mountain Artificial Intelligence Interest Group, I serve on the executive board and lead the education subgroup, helping create a regional forum for educators and practitioners to examine AI with both ambition and rigor. My ASEE papers and presentations document human-reviewed feedback, comparative evaluation of AI-generated engineering work, failure analysis, and AI-integrated computing education.[^6] I also contribute through ASEE review and national conference activity.

I extended that work by creating and teaching three graduate Coursera courses in generative AI. Building a coherent online sequence required deciding what learners most need, how topics should build on one another, and how difficult ideas can be made accessible to an audience beyond one classroom.[^7] The experience influenced my in-person teaching, created reusable public-facing curriculum, and reinforced an important leadership principle for me: expertise becomes more valuable when it is organized so that others can use it.

Invited dissemination has provided another way to extend my work. At the June 2026 Kiewit Faculty Open House, I spoke with approximately three dozen faculty from around the United States about translating AI-in-education deployments into civil-engineering and construction-management programs. Similarly in April 2025, I spoke at the National AI Research Resource (NAIRR) to a national audience of AI practitioners and educators. In May 2023, at Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST), I presented our spin-module educational laboratory work as an international example of collaborative experiential laboratory development.[^8] Additional talks, workshops, podcasts, and professional conversations have allowed lessons from local implementation to contribute to broader discussions in engineering education.

For me, the point of dissemination is not visibility alone. I want to share methods, limitations, and failures so that others can make better decisions. Our early concept-feedback work, for example, showed that some AI outputs were unreliable. Documenting those failures was as important as reporting later improvements. Responsible leadership requires making uncertainty visible, preserving human oversight, and resisting the temptation to present every prototype as a success.

## Extending expertise into the community

I also see public AI literacy as an important form of service. In November 2025, I led “Navigating AI: Build Your Parent Playbook” with the Parent Engagement Network, combining an introduction to generative AI and its implications for parenting with a two-hour guided session using tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, and NotebookLM. I have also offered related programming at the Louisville YMCA and spoken with communities including retirement home residents, Women of Aeronautics and Astronautics, and Front Range Community College.[^9]

My participation in AI advisory groups connected to Boulder Valley School District and the St. Vrain Valley Schools Innovation Center brings a higher-education perspective to local K-12 decisions about AI and learning. Through Science Discovery, I also mentored two high-school students as they used frontier chatbots while working through an advanced aeroelasticity laboratory exercise. They examined both where an AI partner could help and where limited prior knowledge made that partnership unreliable, then presented their work. The outreach experience became an investigation into responsible learning itself, and one of the students earned second place in the Science Discovery presentation awards for her work on the project.[^9]

## Leadership as accountable translation

The scale of my service has changed since my 2023 statement. At that time, much of my leadership centered on departmental curriculum, laboratories, and pandemic response. Today, my work connects Smead Aerospace, CU Boulder, the CU System, regional and national teaching communities, local schools, and public audiences. The underlying commitment has remained the same. I lead by building practical systems, bringing people together around shared problems, and helping communities move from abstract concern to informed action.

My next stage of leadership centers around BOBPE: Bot-Based Personalized Education (pronounced “bah-pee,” /ˈbɑːpi/). It is my north star for a human-on-the-loop ecosystem that can make a class of 200 students feel like a class of 20 and, over time, move large courses closer to a feeling of individualized apprenticeships. [bobpe.com](https://www.bobpe.com) connects the components already deployed, while personalized assignments remain an active area of investigation.[^10] The same advances in AI that make this kind of personalization possible can also strengthen our broader service commitments. For example, I have developed AI-augmented course audits and curriculum-wide semantic search tools that make it more practical to identify gaps, surface connections, and improve courses at scale. My responsibility is to make these efforts durable and measurable, invite others to improve them, and preserve human judgment and student agency as they grow.

Artificial intelligence is changing engineering practice and education quickly. I am excited to help our institutions navigate that change by building useful systems, developing people who can lead independently, and grounding innovation in evidence, responsibility, and the enduring human purposes of education.
