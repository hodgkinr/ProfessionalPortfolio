# Teaching Statement

**Bobby Hodgkinson**

**Working draft — September 8, 2026**

**Target: no more than 1,500 words**

My passion as an educator has always been grounded in experiential learning. Students develop engineering understanding and judgment by building, observing, testing, and reconciling theory with physical evidence.[^1] That philosophy has not changed as generative artificial intelligence (AI) has become part of education and engineering practice. If anything, I think it has become more important.

Easy access to increasingly capable AI changes what it means to learn and contribute, but it does not eliminate the need for wisdom. A phrase I have grown quite fond of is, “Intelligence is the ability to solve problems; wisdom is knowing which problems to solve.” That captures much of what I believe educators are confronting right now. As AI takes on more routine and entry-level work, our students need meaningful experience, judgment, communication skills, responsibility, and ownership earlier in their education. They need to understand the governing concepts, but they also need to know when and how to leverage technology, how to recognize when it is wrong, and when they themselves need to be accountable for the answer.

My pedagogical north star is to make a class of 200 feel, from each student’s perspective, more like a class of 20. Technology can help an instructional team provide timely, individualized feedback at that scale, and I believe it will increasingly allow us to create targeted learning pathways as well. I call this broader effort BOBPE: Bot-Based Personalized Education, (pronounced “bah-pee,” /ˈbɑːpi/), and I share its developing tools and practices at [bobpe.com](https://www.bobpe.com).[^2] The goal is not simply to build another Socratic AI tutor. I am working toward a human-on-the-loop educational ecosystem in which technology expands the attention and personalization we can provide, while instructors remain responsible for the learning environment and students remain responsible for their own work.

## From experience to judgment

That responsibility matters because students can now produce plausible code, polished prose, and convincing plots with almost no friction. Producing an answer is no longer sufficient evidence of learning. Students need to understand the problem, explain and defend their approach, verify their implementation, validate models against reality, and take ownership of the result.

My work in ASEN 2802 provided much of the physical and intellectual foundation for this approach. I led the development and repeated delivery of large-enrollment balloon, aerodynamics, structures, and aeroelasticity lab activities. A recurring lesson was that agreement between models and experiments must be demonstrated rather than assumed. I later extended that idea through the RA.I.phie comparative-analysis framework, where every student received the same AI-generated aeroelasticity lab solution and was asked to interrogate it against theory, boundary conditions, experiments, code, graphs, and their own analysis. Student reflections described numerical comparisons, hand calculations, code and graph inspection, and reconciliation of errors, along with increased confidence evaluating external tools.[^3] The AI artifact did not replace the experiment. It became another model whose credibility had to be established.

I carried the same philosophy into ASEN 1030, the introductory aerospace computing course, but shifted the emphasis from physical experiments to computational reasoning. Students began the course by writing natural-language “recipes” and used generative AI to see how those instructions could become working code. Later in the semester, after developing their own MATLAB skills, they returned to generated code to annotate behavior, debug problems, and explain design decisions. Short one-on-one code interviews were resource intensive, but I considered them essential: students had to explain control flow and reasoning in their own words. An optional practice chatbot gave them unlimited opportunities to rehearse before the consequential human assessment.[^4]

This connects directly to how I talk with students about the profession they are entering. If their contribution is limited to passing a task to an AI system and repeating the output, an employer can get that output without hiring an engineer. Their value has to come from problem formulation, verification and validation, communication, teamwork, and ownership of consequential decisions. Mastery-based grading in ASEN 1030 and ASEN 3501 reinforces that message. Students must demonstrate proficiency on essential outcomes rather than compensate for a fundamental weakness by collecting enough points elsewhere.

I also want students to leave with the expectation that learning continues after the course ends. In ASEN 1030, I integrated self-paced external MATLAB Academy modules with class topics and lab activities. Students received industry-quality training and externally recognized digital credentials they could add to a resume or professional portfolio, while optional modules created pathways into Simulink, data analysis, visualization, and certification preparation.[^4] This was a small but intentional shift in how I think about coursework: when possible, course work should strengthen immediate learning while also showing students how to continue learning and document that growth beyond the university.

## Making in-person learning worth the room

Creating three graduate Coursera courses in generative AI, *Introduction to Generative AI*, *Modern Applications of Generative AI*, and *Advances in Generative AI*, forced me to think carefully about what online instruction does well.[^5] It also made me reconsider what students should gain from being physically present with an instructor and with one another.

If explanations, demonstrations, and high-quality online courses are available at any time, then an in-person class needs to offer more than another set of slides. Its value is in working through difficult ideas with real people: testing an interpretation, exposing a misconception, comparing approaches, asking the question that occurs only after trying something, and getting help while the confusion is still productive.

That idea now shapes ASEN 3501 Experimental Methods, a brand-new course that I designed from the ground up around a flipped, experience-centered model. Students encounter much of the foundational material before class so that our shared time can be used for discussion, problem solving, experiments, feedback, and interaction. The course is organized around six learning outcomes and progresses through execution and uncertainty quantification, predictive modeling and comparative validation, and finally experimental design with verification and validation planning. Students do the experimental work and then defend it through oral assessments, comparative analyses, peer evaluation, and a final oral defense. The [public course site](https://hodgkinr.github.io/CU-Experimental-Methods-Site/index.html), Canvas integration, and [interactive assessment-to-outcome ontology](https://hodgkinr.github.io/CU-Experimental-Methods-Site/interactive_ontologies/index.html) make that progression visible rather than leaving students to infer what the pieces of the course are supposed to add up to.[^6]

## Personalized feedback with human responsibility

The same philosophy guides how I use AI behind the scenes. I am interested in it when it allows an instructional team to provide something better for students, not simply when it allows us to automate something we were already doing.

In Fall 2025 ASEN 1030, for example, my Canvas-integrated workflow helped the grading team return individualized feedback to approximately 100 students within 24 hours. Across the semester, the system processed 969 submissions at a reported total cost of approximately $7.50. It generated course-aligned feedback drafts that human graders reviewed and could revise before release. Median review time was under one minute per submission for the reported graders, and most drafts were released unchanged.[^4][^7] The important result to me was not that AI could generate comments cheaply. It was that students could receive detailed, individualized feedback quickly in a course where doing that manually would otherwise be difficult to sustain.

I am also cautious about treating those successes as evidence that the technology should simply be trusted. In earlier AI-augmented feedback work, I documented serious model-dependent problems; some early outputs were unusable or misleading. We  turned flawed feedback into part of the learning process by asking students to diagnose it.[^8] Later systems improved substantially, but those failures reinforced the importance of human review and student verification. That sequence, from prototype, to identifying failures, to gathering evidence, to revising the practice, is also how I try to approach teaching more generally. Adopting a technology is not the accomplishment. Improving the learning experience is.

My thinking has evolved in another important way as well: student agency. There is a meaningful difference between using AI to help me prepare course materials and using AI to analyze an individual student’s work. In my most recent courses, individualized external AI feedback is therefore opt-in. Participating students’ work is de-identified before being passed to an AI feedback assistant, and the resulting feedback is reviewed by humans before release. Students who do not opt in receive a human-centered alternative with no effect on their grade or standing.[^6][^9] My enthusiasm for a technology should not determine a student’s participation in it.

Building this kind of educational environment is difficult, but I believe it is worth doing. ASEN 2802, ASEN 1030, and ASEN 3501 represent a progression in my own teaching from experiential understanding, to reasoning and validation, to mastery and professional responsibility. BOBPE extends that progression toward personalized education at scale by leveraging modern technology. These technologies will continue to change, probably faster than any of us would like. But my goal for students is more durable: that they leave able to think clearly, learn continuously, work with others, test models against reality, communicate what they know, recognize what they do not know, and always take responsibility for what they create.
