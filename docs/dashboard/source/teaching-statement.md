# Teaching Statement

**Bobby Hodgkinson**

**Working draft — September 7, 2026**

**Target: no more than 1,500 words**

My passion as an educator has always been grounded in experiential learning: students develop engineering understanding and judgment by building, observing, testing, and reconciling theory with physical evidence.[^1] That commitment has led me to explore what I consider the most transformative change to society in my lifetime—the explosive growth of generative artificial intelligence. Easy access to intelligence changes what it means to learn and contribute, but it does not eliminate the need for wisdom. A phrase I've grown quite fond of ``Intelligence is the ability to solve problems; wisdom is knowing which problems to solve" captures the essence of where educators find ourselves right now. As AI takes on more entry-level tasks, higher education must help students develop meaningful experience, judgment, communication, responsibility, and ownership earlier than ever before so they are not only comfortable with the governing concepts but are able to leverage technology.

My north star is to make a class of 200 feel, from each student's perspective, like a class of 20. AI can help an instructional team provide timely, high-quality personalized feedback at that scale. In the near future, I believe it can also help us provide targeted learning pathways and assignments so that a large course feels closer to a one-on-one apprenticeship. I call this project BOBPE: Bot-Based Personalized Education, pronounced similar to my first name—and share its developing tools and practices at [bobpe.com](https://www.bobpe.com).[^2] The aim goes far beyond an AI Socratic tutor. I am working toward an end-to-end, human-on-the-loop educational ecosystem in which technology expands attention and personalization while instructors remain responsible for the learning environment and students remain responsible for their work.

## From experience to judgment

Experiential learning remains essential, but students can now produce plausible code, polished prose, and correct-looking plots with almost no friction. Producing an answer is therefore no longer sufficient evidence of learning. Students must understand the problem, explain and defend their approach, verify implementation, validate models against reality, and accept responsibility for the result.

ASEN 2802 established the physical and intellectual foundation for this approach. I led the development and repeated delivery of large-enrollment balloon, aerodynamics, structures, and aeroelasticity laboratories. Students learned that agreement between models and experiments must be demonstrated rather than assumed. I later extended that habit of mind through the RA.I.phie comparative-analysis framework. Where I provided every student the same AI-generated aeroelasticity lab solution and they had to interrogate it against theory, boundary conditions, experiments, code, graphs, and their own analysis. Using voluntary reflections, students described numerical comparisons, hand checks, code and graph inspection, and error reconciliation, along with increased confidence evaluating external tools.[^3] The AI artifact did not replace the experiment; it became another model whose credibility had to be established.

ASEN 1030 translated this philosophy into introductory aerospace computing by moving beyond teaching coding syntax. In the first lab, students begin with natural-language “recipes” and used generative AI to see how their instructions could become code. They then return to that code later in the semester after they've become familiar with MATLAB to annotate program behavior, debug, and explain design decisions. In the class, short, one-on-one code interviews were resource intensive but essential: students had to explain control flow and reasoning in their own words. An optional practice chatbot offered unlimited formative rehearsal before the consequential human assessment.[^4]

These assessments make the professional stakes explicit. If a student's contribution is limited to passing a task to an AI system and repeating its output, an employer can obtain that output without hiring an engineer. The student's value must lie in sound problem formulation, verification and validation, communication, teamwork, and ownership of consequential decisions. Mastery-based grading in ASEN 1030 and ASEN 3501 reinforces this message: students must demonstrate proficiency on essential outcomes rather than compensate for a critical weakness by accumulating points elsewhere.

I also want students to see formal education as the beginning of lifelong learning, not its endpoint. In ASEN 1030, I integrated self-paced MATLAB Academy modules with class topics and Canvas. This gave students industry-quality training and externally recognized digital credentials they could add to a resume or professional portfolio; optional advanced modules provided pathways into Simulink, data analysis, visualization, and certification preparation. At a time when students and families are weighing the value of higher education, those credentials provide evidence of learning beyond the diploma. This was my first small-scale attempt to make “homework” serve a second purpose: strengthening course learning while showing students how to find and document learning opportunities beyond the university.[^4]

## Making in-person learning worth the room

Creating three graduate Coursera courses in generative AI—*Introduction to Generative AI*, *Modern Applications of Generative AI*, and *Advances in Generative AI*—improved how I organize and deliver in-person technical content.[^5] It also sharpened a distinction that now shapes ASEN 3501. When students can access explanations, demonstrations, and high-quality online courses at any time, an in-person class must offer significantly more than slides and a textbook. Its distinctive value is the experience of working through difficult ideas with real people: testing an interpretation, exposing a misconception, comparing approaches, and receiving help at the moment confusion becomes productive.

I have therefore designed ASEN 3501 Experimental Methods toward a flipped, experience-centered format. Students encounter foundational content before class; reserving our shared time for discussion, problem solving, experimental work, feedback, and the human interaction needed to crystallize ideas they will use in the lab and their careers. The course is organized around six learning outcomes and three phases: execution and quantification; predictive modeling and comparative validation; and experimental design with verification and validation planning. Students produce experimental work and defend it through oral assessments, comparative analysis, peer evaluation, and a final oral defense. The [public course site](https://hodgkinr.github.io/CU-Experimental-Methods-Site/index.html), Canvas integration, and an interactive assessment-to-outcome ontology make the structure visible to students and collaborators.[^6]

## Personalized feedback with human responsibility

I use generative AI when it expands what an instructional team can provide while preserving human judgment. In Fall 2025 duing ASEN 1030, my Canvas-integrated workflow helped the grading team return individualized feedback to approximately 100 students within 24 hours. Across the semester, the system processed 969 submissions at a reported total cost of approximately $7.50. It generated course-aligned drafts, while human graders reviewed and could revise every response before release. Median review time was under one minute per submission for the reported graders, and most drafts were released unchanged.[^4][^7] AI made rapid, detailed, personalized feedback feasible at scale; it did not make the consequential decision.

Those results do not justify blind trust. In earlier AI-augmented feedback work, I documented serious model-dependent issues; early outputs were often unusable or misleading. Later models improved substantially, but the study reinforced the need for human review and student verification. We also turned flawed feedback into a learning opportunity by asking students to diagnose it.[^8] This sequence—from prototype, to failure analysis, to measured improvement, to redesigned practice—captures how I reflect on teaching. Technology adoption is not the accomplishment. The accomplishment is a better learning experience supported by evidence, honest attention to limitations, and continued revision.

My thinking has also evolved around student agency. There is an important difference between using AI to help prepare course materials and using it to analyze an individual student's work. In my most recent courses, individualized external AI feedback is therefore opt-in. Participating students' work is de-identified before it is passed to an AI feedback assistant and is reviewed by humans before release. Students who do not opt in receive a human-centered alternative with no effect on their grade or standing.[^6][^9] My enthusiasm for the technology should not determine a student's participation.

Building this kind of course is difficult. I believe it is worth doing, that technology can make the hardest parts more practical, and that teaching faculty should help lead this new frontier. ASEN 2802, ASEN 1030, and ASEN 3501 represent a progression from experiential understanding to reasoning, validation, and mastery. BOBPE extends that progression toward personalized education at scale. The aim remains human: engineers who can think clearly, learn continuously, work with others, validate models with real data, communicate what they know, and take responsibility for what they create.
