# Teaching Statement

**Bobby Hodgkinson**  
**Working draft — August 31, 2026**  
**Target: no more than 1,500 words**

My passion as an educator has always been grounded in experiential learning: students develop engineering understanding and judgment by building, observing, testing, and reconciling theory with physical evidence.[^1] That commitment has led me to explore what I consider the most transformative change to society in my lifetime—the explosive growth of generative artificial intelligence. Easy access to intelligence changes what it means to learn and contribute, but it does not eliminate the need for wisdom. Intelligence is the ability to solve problems; wisdom is knowing which problems to solve. We must convey that distinction to our students. As AI takes on more entry-level tasks, higher education must help students develop meaningful experience, judgment, communication, responsibility, and ownership earlier.

My guiding north star for AI in education is to enhance personalized education at scale while preserving the human responsibility at the center of learning. My teaching has developed around two connected foundations in pursuit of that aim. The first is **durable curriculum leadership** across a sequence: ASEN 2802 established large-scale experiential curriculum; ASEN 1030 redesigned introductory computing around reasoning and authentic assessment; and ASEN 3501 carries those principles into model validation and experimental methods. The second is **responsible AI augmentation with measured results**. I have built, deployed, measured, criticized, improved, and governed AI-enabled teaching systems while keeping humans responsible and giving students increasing agency over individualized AI use. Together, these foundations develop engineers who can use powerful tools without surrendering understanding or accountability.

## Durable curriculum leadership: from experience to judgment

Experiential learning remains essential, but the environment in which students develop engineering intuition has changed. Students can now produce plausible code, polished prose, and correct-looking plots with almost no friction. Producing an answer is therefore no longer sufficient evidence of learning. Students must understand the problem, explain and defend their approach, verify implementation, validate models against reality, communicate clearly, and accept responsibility for the result.

ASEN 2802 established the physical and intellectual foundation for this approach. I led the development and repeated delivery of large-enrollment balloon, aerodynamics, structures, and aeroelasticity laboratories. Students learned that agreement between models and experiments must be demonstrated rather than assumed. I later extended that habit of mind through the RA.I.phie comparative-analysis framework. Instead of asking students to obtain an answer from a chatbot, I gave every student the same AI-generated aeroelasticity solution and required them to interrogate it against theory, boundary conditions, experiments, code, graphs, and their own analysis. Across 315 students, 97 voluntary reflections described numerical comparisons, hand checks, code and graph inspection, and error reconciliation, along with increased confidence evaluating external tools.[^2] The AI artifact did not replace the experiment; it gave students another model whose credibility had to be established.

ASEN 1030 translated this philosophy into introductory aerospace computing by moving beyond syntax. Students began with natural-language “recipes” and used generative AI to see how their own instructions could become code. They then returned to that code to annotate program behavior, answer weekly concept questions, debug, and explain design decisions. Live code interviews were resource intensive but essential: students had to explain control flow and reasoning in their own words, without hiding behind generated output. An optional practice chatbot provided unlimited formative rehearsal and helped students build confidence before the consequential human assessment.[^3]

The course shifts from “just in case” toward “just in time” learning without abandoning fundamentals. Cheap computation and generated explanations make core concepts more important, not less. Students need enough structure to formulate problems, recognize invalid assumptions, and judge whether outputs are credible. The aim is to build durable principles, learn new tools responsibly, and recognize when deeper expertise remains essential.

I reinforce that aim through mastery-based grading in ASEN 1030 and ASEN 3501. Students must demonstrate proficiency on key milestones rather than compensate for a critical weakness by accumulating points elsewhere. Higher grades recognize performance beyond the minimum standard on major assessments and additional work such as final projects. This more closely resembles professional practice: engineers must meet essential requirements, while exceptional performance, initiative, and the ability to extend the work are rewarded.

ASEN 3501 Experimental Methods brings the full arc together. The Fall 2026 course is organized around six learning outcomes and three phases: execution and quantification; predictive modeling and comparative validation; and experimental design with verification and validation planning. Students produce experimental work and then defend it through oral assessments, comparative analysis, peer evaluation, and a final oral defense. A public course source, Canvas integration, and an interactive assessment-to-outcome ontology make the structure visible to students and collaborators.[^4] The result is not a collection of isolated assignments. It is a progression in which students accumulate evidence that they can conduct, evaluate, communicate, and ultimately design experimental work.

## Responsible AI augmentation: expand capacity, preserve responsibility

I make the professional stakes explicit with students. I ask what value they will bring to a future employer if their contribution is limited to passing a task to an AI system and regurgitating its output. “I can run an agent in my office for pennies a day,” I tell them; the value of an engineer must lie elsewhere. This is not a message of fear or a strategy for catching students who “cheat.” It is an invitation to aim higher. My assessments are designed to show what students can do and to develop what future colleagues and employers will value: clear communication, sound problem formulation, verification and validation, teamwork, confidence grounded in evidence, and ownership of consequential decisions.

I use generative AI when it expands what an instructional team can provide while preserving human judgment. In ASEN 1030, my Canvas-integrated workflow helped the grading team return individualized feedback to approximately 100 students within 24 hours. Across the semester, the system processed 969 submissions at a reported total cost of approximately $7.50. It generated course-aligned drafts, but human graders reviewed and could revise every response before release. Median review time was under one minute per submission for the reported graders, and most drafts were released unchanged.[^3][^5] AI made rapid, detailed, personalized feedback feasible at scale; it did not make the consequential decision.

Those results do not justify blind trust. In earlier AI-augmented feedback work, I evaluated generated concept questions and individualized responses and documented serious model-dependent issues. Early outputs were often unusable or misleading. Later models improved substantially, but the study reinforced the need for human review and student verification. We also turned flawed feedback into a learning opportunity by asking students to diagnose it.[^6] This sequence—from prototype, to failure analysis, to measured improvement, to redesigned practice—captures how I reflect on teaching. Technology adoption is not the accomplishment. The accomplishment is a better learning experience supported by evidence, honest attention to limitations, and continued revision.

My thinking has also evolved around student agency. There is an important difference between using AI to help me prepare course materials and using it to analyze an individual student's work. In my most recent courses, individualized external AI feedback is therefore opt-in. For participating students, work is de-identified before it is passed to an AI feedback assistant and is reviewed by humans before release. Students who do not opt in receive a human-centered alternative with no effect on their grade or standing.[^4][^7] My work shows that AI can support an efficient pipeline for high-quality personalized feedback, but my enthusiasm for the technology should not automatically determine a student's participation.

I do not claim to have solved the engineering educational challenges created by AI. I am trying to get closer through disciplined practice: build authentic experiences, measure what happens, acknowledge failure, preserve human responsibility, and share what works. ASEN 2802, ASEN 1030, and ASEN 3501 represent a durable progression from experiential understanding to reasoning, validation, and mastery. AI provides leverage around that progression, but the aim remains human: engineers who can think clearly, learn continuously, work with others, validate models with real data, communicate what they know, and take responsibility for what they create.
