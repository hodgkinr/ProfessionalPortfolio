## Nomination Narrative

### A description of the specific AI use in the course(s)

I am nominating my work integrating generative AI into two required Aerospace Engineering courses, ASEN 1030 (Computing with MATLAB) and ASEN 2802 (Aerospace Sciences Lab). In both courses, I use AI to strengthen students’ ability to reason, validate, and communicate, rather than treating AI as a shortcut to produce answers. The central idea is that students must learn how to evaluate outputs that can look polished and confident but may still be wrong, and they must be able to explain why they trust a result.

In ASEN 1030, the first major activity is a “recipe” exercise designed to demystify the connection between everyday language and computer code. Before students see any code at all, they write a step-by-step recipe, in their own natural language, for building a grade calculator based on the syllabus. The goal is to build curiosity and exploration by letting students see that their own words can become a working program, and that programming begins with decomposition and logic, not syntax. Students then use generative AI to translate their recipe into MATLAB code. Later in the semester, after building an understanding of syntax they revisit the AI-generated code. Students interpret it, annotate it, test it, and explain what it is doing, where it might be confusing, and how they would improve it.

To keep assessment authentic in a world where code generation is readily available, I use short code interviews where students explain control flow, correctness, and debugging decisions using both provided code and their own code. I provided a custom chatbot to the class to help them receive constructive feedback and unlimited practice on these interviews. I also use weekly short-answer concept questions supported by an AI-augmented feedback workflow. AI drafts personalized feedback, but human graders reviewing responses before feedback is released. This gives the ability for students to receive timely, personalized guidance at scale without removing instructor responsibility for accuracy and grading.

In ASEN 2802, I used AI as a structured vehicle for teaching validation and verification in model-based design and experimental interpretation. Students received standardized, instructor-supplied AI-generated solution artifacts for lab experiment analysis milestones. Their task was to evaluate those artifacts by comparing them against theory, boundary conditions, physical reasoning, and evidence from their own work. They identified where the AI solution was correct, where it was incorrect, and most importantly they were able to explain why. Every student critiqued the same AI artifacts, which supports equity and created a shared basis for discussion.

### Demonstration of the increase in student performance relative to the learning goals/outcomes of the course(s)

In ASEN 1030, students were expected to develop computational thinking, implement core programming constructs, and develop, test, and debug MATLAB programs for aerospace applications. The recipe activity directly targets computational thinking by allowing students to decompose a familiar, real task into an algorithm before they've ever seen syntax or even know the details of what makes up an algorithm. Students saw their plan become code, which helps them connect abstraction and algorithm development to program behavior. When students reflected on the course overall, 67 percent mentioned this exercise improved their understanding of code logic and flow, and 61 percent emphasized it improved their step-by-step logical thinking. These outcomes align directly with the objective of applying computational thinking principles.

To measure understanding in a way that cannot be reduced to producing a working script, the in-person code interview format required students to explain control flow and reasoning in real time. For the objective tied to developing, testing, and debugging, the weekly concept questions and feedback workflow strengthen the learning cycle by enabling prompt, specific feedback on misconceptions and reasoning. In implementing this workflow, the grading team processed 969 student submissions while maintaining a median human review time under one minute per response, ranging from 0.44 to 0.91 minutes depending on grader. For four graders, approximately 75 to 80 percent of AI-drafted feedback was released unchanged after review, with edits made when needed. Across the dataset, the median throughput was approximately 84 words of feedback per grader-minute - effectively turning the grading team into advanced typists providing pedagogically sound personalized constructive feedback to ~100 students the next day.

In ASEN 2802, students were expected to build competency in model-based design and in interpreting experimental data using theory across statics, structural mechanics, aerodynamics, and thermodynamics. The AI critique framework developed these competencies by requiring students to validate and verify rather than accept an answer at face value. In a class of 315 students, 97 students submitted volunteer reflections describing how they evaluated AI-generated solutions. In those reflections, students documented validation behaviors that match the course goals, including comparing numerical values, comparing trends in plots, performing hand calculations, checking boundary conditions, inspecting code structure and logic, and using deviation metrics to quantify differences. Students also described deeper understanding of boundary conditions and load distributions and improved critical thinking through debugging and error analysis. ### Demonstration of the impact the specific AI use has had on student learning beyond the nominee’s course(s)

These approaches were built to be transferable. The ASEN 1030 structure uses students’ own natural language as a bridge into programming, a pattern that can be applied across introductory computing contexts. The ASEN 2802 structure uses standardized AI-generated artifacts as objects for critique, a pattern that can be used in any course where verification and judgment are central. Both approaches teach students habits that generalize beyond a single assignment: explain your reasoning, test your assumptions, and verify results using evidence.
